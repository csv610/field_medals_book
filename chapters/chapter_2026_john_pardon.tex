\section{Problem History}

Knot theory studies the embedding of circles in three-dimensional space. A fundamental question in metric geometry, posed by Mikhail Gromov, concerns \textbf{knot distortion}: for a knotted curve in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the distortion is the supremum over all pairs of points on the curve of the ratio of arc-length distance to ambient Euclidean distance. A round circle has distortion 1, while more complex knots can have higher distortion. Gromov asked whether there exist knots with arbitrarily large distortion---a question that had been open for 25 years when Pardon solved it as an undergraduate.

The \textbf{Hilbert--Smith conjecture} asks whether a locally compact group that acts faithfully by homeomorphisms on a manifold must be a Lie group. Equivalently, every locally compact, totally disconnected topological group acting faithfully on a manifold is discrete. This is one of the fundamental questions in geometric topology, connecting group theory, topology, and dynamics. The conjecture had been open since the 1930s.

The Hilbert--Smith conjecture is closely related to the question of whether totally disconnected groups can act ``wildly'' on manifolds. The $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$ provide the key test case: the conjecture is equivalent to the statement that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ cannot act faithfully by homeomorphisms on any manifold. If such an action existed, it would produce a continuous homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}_p$ to the homeomorphism group of the manifold, which is a ``wild'' object that cannot arise from a Lie group action.

The difficulty of the conjecture lies in the fact that homeomorphisms can be much more flexible than diffeomorphisms. While it is relatively easy to show that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ cannot act faithfully by diffeomorphisms on a manifold (because the group of diffeomorphisms has a Lie group structure), the group of homeomorphisms is much larger and has no such structure. Pardon's breakthrough was to show that for 3-manifolds, the extra flexibility of homeomorphisms is not enough to allow a faithful action of $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

In symplectic geometry, the \textbf{Gromov--Witten invariants} are defined by counting pseudo-holomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold $(M,\omega)$ satisfying boundary or incidence conditions. A pseudo-holomorphic curve is a map $u:(\Sigma,j)\to(M,\omega)$ satisfying the Cauchy--Riemann equation $\bar{\partial}_J u = 0$. However, the moduli space of such curves is often singular or of the wrong dimension, making a naive count impossible.

The standard approach is to construct a \textbf{virtual fundamental cycle}: a homology class in the moduli space that plays the role of the fundamental class if the space were smooth and of the expected dimension. Previous constructions (by Li--Tian, Behrend--Fantechi, Fukaya--Ono, and others) worked in specific settings but had limitations.

\textbf{Fukaya categories} are $A_\infty$-categories associated to a symplectic manifold $(M,\omega)$, whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds $L \subset M$ and whose morphisms $\mathrm{hom}(L_0,L_1)$ are generated by intersection points, with composition defined by counting pseudo-holomorphic disks. They play a central role in homological mirror symmetry (Kontsevich, 1994), connecting symplectic geometry to algebraic geometry through the mirror symmetry correspondence.

However, foundational issues---particularly in the construction of virtual fundamental cycles for moduli spaces of holomorphic curves in Liouville manifolds (exact symplectic manifolds with contact-type boundary)---had remained unresolved. These manifolds arise naturally as complements of hypersurfaces in projective space and as Stein manifolds.

\section{How Was It Solved}

As an undergraduate at Princeton, Pardon solved Gromov's distortion problem by proving that for any constant $C$, there exist knots whose distortion exceeds $C$. The proof constructs explicit families of knots with controlled geometry, showing that the ratio of arc-length to ambient distance can be made arbitrarily large using techniques from geometric measure theory and the analysis of embeddings.

Pardon proved the Hilbert--Smith conjecture for ``rough'' (non-differentiable) group actions on 3-manifolds: if a totally disconnected, locally compact group acts faithfully by homeomorphisms on a 3-manifold, then the group must be discrete. This was the first case of the Hilbert--Smith conjecture proved for actions on manifolds of any dimension. The proof uses contact-local isotopy techniques and the theory of group actions on low-dimensional manifolds.

He also proved Edmonds' smoothing conjecture for rough group actions on 3-manifolds, showing that such actions can be approximated by smooth actions. This complements the Hilbert--Smith result and provides insight into the relationship between topology and dynamics.

For symplectic geometry, Pardon developed new methods for constructing virtual fundamental cycles on moduli spaces of pseudo-holomorphic curves, particularly for Fukaya categories of Liouville manifolds. His approach introduced innovative techniques from derived algebraic geometry and moduli theory. The key idea is to construct a \emph{derived enhancement} of the moduli problem, replacing the naive moduli space with a derived stack that carries a perfect obstruction theory. The virtual fundamental cycle is then constructed using the techniques of virtual fundamental classes from algebraic geometry.

Pardon made fundamental contributions to the foundations of Fukaya categories for Liouville manifolds, resolving long-standing foundational issues and introducing new techniques for counting holomorphic curves. His work has been essential for the development of wrapped Floer theory and its applications to mirror symmetry. The construction provides rigorous definitions and well-defined invariants where previously only formal or heuristic constructions existed.

The proofs are notable for their technical precision and generality. Pardon's virtual fundamental cycle construction works in greater generality than previous approaches, covering settings where the moduli space is highly singular and the obstruction theory is not perfect in the traditional sense.

\section{Impact of This Discovery}

Pardon's work on knot distortion settled a fundamental question in metric geometry, showing that the geometry of knotted curves in three-dimensional space can be far more complex than naive intuition suggests. This result connects knot theory to bi-Lipschitz embeddings and coarse geometry.

The Hilbert--Smith conjecture for 3-manifolds is a major advance in geometric topology. While the full conjecture in higher dimensions remains open, Pardon's result demonstrates that the conjecture is true in the lowest interesting dimension and provides techniques that may be applicable more broadly.

The new construction of virtual fundamental cycles has transformed the study of Gromov--Witten invariants and Fukaya categories. It provides a rigorous foundation for enumerative geometry in symplectic settings that were previously inaccessible, with applications to homological mirror symmetry and the study of Lagrangian submanifolds.

Pardon's contributions to the foundations of Fukaya categories have resolved long-standing foundational issues that had hindered progress in symplectic topology. His work has enabled new results on wrapped Floer theory, Lagrangian embeddings, and the symplectic topology of Liouville manifolds.

His methods have been adopted by researchers worldwide and have opened new directions of inquiry in symplectic geometry, particularly in the study of moduli problems with non-trivial obstructions.

The \textbf{Gromov--Witten invariants} are defined by counting pseudo-holomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ satisfying boundary or incidence conditions. For a compact symplectic manifold of dimension $2n$, the Gromov--Witten invariant of class $A \in H_2(M)$ and genus $g$ is defined by
\[
\langle \gamma_1, \ldots, \gamma_k \rangle_{g,A} = \int_{[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}(M,A)]^{vir}} \mathrm{ev}_1^*(\gamma_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge \mathrm{ev}_k^*(\gamma_k),
\]
where $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}(M,A)$ is the moduli space of stable maps of genus $g$ with $k$ marked points in class $A$, and $[\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}(M,A)]^{vir}$ is the virtual fundamental cycle. The virtual fundamental cycle is needed because the moduli space is typically singular and of the wrong dimension, making a naive count impossible.

Pardon's construction of the virtual fundamental cycle uses derived algebraic geometry to replace the naive moduli space with a derived stack that carries a perfect obstruction theory. The derived stack encodes the full deformation theory of the moduli problem, and the virtual fundamental cycle is constructed using the techniques of virtual fundamental classes from algebraic geometry. This approach works in greater generality than previous constructions, covering settings where the moduli space is highly singular and the obstruction theory is not perfect in the traditional sense.

The \textbf{derived enhancement} of the moduli problem replaces the naive moduli space $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}(M,A)$ with a derived stack $\mathbb{R}\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,k}(M,A)$ that carries a perfect obstruction theory. The derived stack encodes the full deformation theory of the moduli problem, including higher obstructions that are not visible in the classical moduli space. The virtual fundamental cycle is then constructed using the techniques of virtual fundamental classes from algebraic geometry: one takes the intersection product of the zero sections of the obstruction bundle with the fundamental class of the moduli space, which gives a homology class in the expected dimension.

The key advantage of Pardon's approach is that it works for Liouville manifolds, where previous constructions had limitations. A Liouville manifold is an exact symplectic manifold $(M, \omega = d\lambda)$ with a contact-type boundary, and the Fukaya category of a Liouville manifold is defined using wrapped Floer theory. The virtual fundamental cycle construction for Liouville manifolds is more delicate than for compact manifolds because the moduli spaces are non-compact and the obstruction theory may not be perfect in the traditional sense. Pardon's derived enhancement provides a framework for handling these difficulties.

The connection between derived algebraic geometry and symplectic topology in Pardon's work has led to new computational techniques for Gromov--Witten invariants. The derived enhancement of the moduli problem provides a systematic way to compute virtual fundamental cycles, and the resulting algorithms have been implemented in computer algebra systems. These computations have led to new results on the enumerative geometry of symplectic manifolds and have provided evidence for conjectures in mirror symmetry.

The connection between symplectic geometry and algebraic geometry through Pardon's work has led to new results on the enumerative geometry of both fields. The Gromov--Witten invariants of a symplectic manifold are related to the enumerative invariants of its mirror variety, and Pardon's construction of virtual fundamental cycles provides a rigorous framework for understanding this relationship. The derived enhancement of the moduli problem has also led to new results on the structure of Gromov--Witten invariants, including new recursion relations and functional equations.

\section{Modern Views and Improvements}

Major open questions include the full Hilbert--Smith conjecture in higher dimensions, the construction of Fukaya categories for more general symplectic manifolds, and the development of new invariants from the count of holomorphic curves.

Pardon's virtual fundamental cycle construction has been extended and refined by other researchers, leading to new applications in enumerative geometry and the study of Donaldson--Thomas type invariants in symplectic settings.

The connection between derived algebraic geometry and symplectic topology, pioneered in Pardon's work, has become an active area of research. New techniques from derived and higher categorical methods continue to find applications in the construction and computation of symplectic invariants.

Future directions include the development of Floer-theoretic invariants for more exotic symplectic manifolds, the application of Pardon's techniques to contact topology and Legendrian knots, and the exploration of connections between symplectic geometry and quantum field theory.

The full Hilbert--Smith conjecture in higher dimensions remains one of the most important open problems in geometric topology. While Pardon's result for 3-manifolds is the strongest known case, the conjecture in dimensions 4 and above requires fundamentally new techniques. The difficulty is that in higher dimensions, homeomorphisms are even more flexible than in dimension 3, making it harder to rule out wild group actions.

The development of computational tools for Gromov--Witten invariants, based on Pardon's virtual fundamental cycle construction, has led to new enumerative results. These computations have confirmed predictions from mirror symmetry and have provided new data for testing conjectures in symplectic topology.

The study of Fukaya categories for Liouville manifolds has been further developed using Pardon's foundational work. The wrapped Fukaya category, which includes non-compact Lagrangians, has been computed in several examples, including cotangent bundles of spheres and symplectic resolutions. These computations have verified predictions from homological mirror symmetry and have provided new insights into the structure of Lagrangian submanifolds.

The connection between symplectic geometry and contact topology has been deepened by Pardon's work on the Hilbert--Smith conjecture. The techniques used to prove the conjecture for 3-manifolds have been adapted to study contact structures on 3-manifolds, leading to new results on the classification of contact structures and on the relationship between contact topology and knot theory. The study of Legendrian knots and their invariants is an active area that draws on Pardon's techniques.

The development of derived algebraic geometry has provided new tools for constructing virtual fundamental cycles in settings beyond those covered by Pardon's original work. The derived enhancement of moduli problems, which Pardon introduced for symplectic geometry, has been applied to algebraic geometry and to the theory of Donaldson-Thomas invariants. These applications demonstrate the versatility of Pardon's approach and its potential for further development in other areas of geometry.

The study of knot invariants, motivated by Pardon's work on knot distortion, continues to be an active area. The distortion of a knot is a metric invariant that measures the ratio of arc-length distance to ambient Euclidean distance, and it provides information about the geometry of the knot. The study of knot distortion and related invariants has connections to the theory of bi-Lipschitz embeddings, to geometric measure theory, and to the study of fractal geometry in three dimensions.

The connection between Pardon's work on virtual fundamental cycles and the theory of Donaldson-Thomas invariants in algebraic geometry is an active area of research. Donaldson-Thomas invariants are defined by counting sheaves on Calabi-Yau manifolds, and the construction of the virtual fundamental cycle is essential for making this count rigorous. Pardon's derived enhancement of the moduli problem provides a framework for constructing Donaldson-Thomas invariants in settings where the classical approach fails, and the connection between symplectic and algebraic geometry through derived methods continues to develop.

The study of contact structures on 3-manifolds has been influenced by Pardon's work on the Hilbert-Smith conjecture. Contact structures are geometric structures on odd-dimensional manifolds that generalize the notion of a hyperplane distribution, and their classification is a central problem in contact topology. The techniques developed by Pardon for studying homeomorphism groups of 3-manifolds have been applied to the classification of contact structures, leading to new results on the relationship between contact topology and the topology of the underlying manifold.

The study of Legendrian knots, which are knots that are everywhere tangent to a contact structure, is an active area that draws on Pardon's techniques. Legendrian knots carry additional geometric information beyond their topological type, and their invariants---such as the Thurston-Bennequin number and the rotation number---are important tools in contact topology. The construction of new invariants for Legendrian knots, using techniques from symplectic geometry and Floer theory, is an active area that builds on Pardon's foundational work.

The study of exact Lagrangian submanifolds in symplectic manifolds is closely related to Pardon's work on Fukaya categories. Exact Lagrangian submanifolds are Lagrangian submanifolds on which the symplectic form vanishes, and their existence and classification are central problems in symplectic topology. The Fukaya category, whose objects are Lagrangian submanifolds, provides a framework for studying exact Lagrangians, and Pardon's work on the foundations of Fukaya categories has led to new results on the classification of exact Lagrangians in Liouville manifolds.

The \textbf{wrapped Fukaya category} is a variant of the Fukaya category that includes non-compact Lagrangians and uses a different Hamiltonian perturbation to define the morphism complexes. The wrapped Fukaya category is expected to be equivalent to the category of coherent sheaves on the mirror Landau--Ginzburg model, which is a generalization of mirror symmetry to non-compact settings. Pardon's work provides the foundations for the wrapped Fukaya category in Liouville manifolds, where the contact boundary provides a natural setting for the wrapping procedure.

The connection between symplectic geometry and quantum field theory is an active area of research. The path integral of a topological quantum field theory is expected to produce symplectic invariants (such as Gromov--Witten invariants), and the axioms of quantum field theory provide constraints on the structure of symplectic invariants. Pardon's work on virtual fundamental cycles provides a rigorous framework for defining these invariants, and the connection to quantum field theory may lead to new computational techniques.

The development of \textbf{categorical} invariants in symplectic geometry is another direction. The Fukaya category is an example of a categorical invariant, and there are expectations that other categorical structures (such as $A_\infty$-categories and $L_\infty$-algebras) will provide new invariants for symplectic manifolds. Pardon's work on the foundations of Fukaya categories provides a template for developing these categorical invariants.
